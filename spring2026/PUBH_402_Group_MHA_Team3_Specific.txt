Title: MHA-T3-1 Map — workflow framing
Points: 2
1.  Your team's final system map explicitly framed the topic around "capacity, workforce, referrals, and administrative processes as key drivers of access to mental health services." From a systems perspective, what is the most defensible critique of this framing as the central organizing logic of your map?
a)  Workforce and capacity have no relationship to access.
*b) The framing emphasizes the *delivery side* of the system — what happens to patients once they engage with care — but underrepresents the *demand-shaping* features (uninsured population size, out-of-pocket costs, structural inequities beyond segregation, stigma, and the political-economic conditions of mental health funding) that determine whether and how patients arrive in the system at all. The map can analyze how care is delivered but is structurally limited in explaining why so many people who need care never engage.
c)  The framing is too broad and includes too many elements.
d)  The framing makes the map invalid.

Title: MHA-T3-2 Map — single balancing loop
Points: 2
1.  Your team's final system map identified one feedback loop, B1 "Administrative Capacity Constraint," in which specialty care visit capacity and administrative processing capacity affect each other in a balancing relationship. The map does not identify any reinforcing loops. From a systems perspective, what does the absence of identified reinforcing loops most clearly suggest about the map?
a)  Reinforcing loops are not relevant to mental health access.
*b) Real systems typically contain both reinforcing and balancing loops operating simultaneously, and the absence of identified reinforcing loops is a signal that either (a) the map's elements have been chosen in a way that does not surface reinforcing dynamics, or (b) reinforcing loops exist in the system but were not identified — either way, the map is structurally limited because it cannot explain why mental health access disparities *escalate* over time, only why they remain constrained at a given level.
c)  Maps with only balancing loops are more accurate than maps with reinforcing loops.
d)  The map should have included no loops at all.

Title: MHA-T3-3 Map — exclusion of uninsured population
Points: 2
1.  Your team's element reconsideration explicitly excluded "uninsured population size" from the system map, with the team's reasoning stating that insurance status affects affordability and utilization but does not directly change the number of available providers or appointment slots. From a systems perspective, what is the most defensible critique of this exclusion?
a)  Uninsured population size is not part of the U.S. health care system.
*b) The exclusion treats system capacity as if it were independent of who the patient population is, but provider behavior is shaped by payer mix — the share of uninsured patients in a population affects which providers locate there, which providers accept patients there, and what services are offered. Capacity and insurance status are interrelated through provider response to reimbursement environment, which the map does not represent because both ends of that pathway are absent or weakly developed.
c)  The team should have included every possible element.
d)  Insurance status has no relationship to mental health access.

Title: MHA-T3-4 Map — administrative processing as central element
Points: 2
1.  Your team's final system map placed administrative processing capacity as a central element with bidirectional links to specialty care visit capacity, forming the balancing loop B1. The map describes administrative processes — scheduling, prior authorization, claims handling, documentation — as determining "how quickly patients can move through the system." From a systems perspective, what is the most defensible critique of treating administrative processing capacity as the central driver of access in this way?
a)  Administrative processing has no effect on access.
*b) Administrative friction is a real and important constraint, but the framing treats it as a relatively neutral capacity issue rather than as a *designed* feature of the system that serves specific functions (cost containment for payers, gatekeeping for managed care, documentation for accountability). Without representing the structural reasons administrative friction exists, the map suggests that increasing administrative capacity is the relevant lever, when in many cases the binding constraint is the policy and payer incentives that *produce* administrative friction in the first place.
c)  Prior authorization is not a real administrative requirement.
d)  Administrative processes should not appear in any system map.
