Title: MHA01 Coverage vs access
Points: 2
1.  Following expansions of mental health parity laws and Medicaid coverage of behavioral health services, surveys consistently show that a majority of adults with a diagnosed mental illness still do not receive any treatment in a given year. Which of the following are consistent with a systems-level account of why coverage gains have not closed the treatment gap? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Specialty mental health workforce supply is geographically maldistributed, leaving large areas without accessible providers.
[*] A substantial share of psychiatrists and therapists do not accept insurance — particularly Medicaid — limiting effective access for insured patients.
[*] Stigma, work and childcare schedules, and prior negative experiences with the system shape care-seeking independently of coverage.
[ ] Mental illness has no relationship to access barriers.
[*] Parity laws regulate plan design but enforcement of their behavioral-medical equivalence has been uneven, allowing access asymmetries to persist.

Title: MHA02 Workforce and specialty supply
Points: 2
1.  A state projects a substantial shortage of psychiatrists relative to mental health care need over the next decade. The legislature responds by funding 30 additional psychiatry residency slots concentrated at the state's academic medical center in its largest urban area. Five years later, psychiatry supply in rural and underserved counties is essentially unchanged. From a systems perspective, the most defensible explanation is:
a)  Funding residency slots has no effect on overall psychiatry supply.
*b) Residency location strongly predicts eventual practice location, and the distribution of new slots determines the geographic distribution of future supply; expanding training capacity without changing the structural conditions that shape where psychiatrists choose to practice (reimbursement, support infrastructure, scope of practice, community resources) leaves the rural-urban gap unchanged.
c)  Rural areas no longer need psychiatric services.
d)  Federal regulations prohibit psychiatrists from practicing rurally.

Title: MHA03 Fee-for-service and care coordination
Points: 2
1.  A primary care practice identifies depression in many of its patients but reports difficulty connecting them to mental health treatment. The practice operates under predominantly fee-for-service payment. From a systems perspective, which of the following are reasonable explanations of how the payment structure affects this outcome? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Care coordination activities — phone calls, warm handoffs, follow-up tracking — are often not billable under fee-for-service, reducing the practice's incentive to invest in them.
[*] Brief screening can be billed but the time-intensive work of getting patients into specialty care often cannot, creating a structural mismatch between what is reimbursed and what produces access.
[ ] Fee-for-service requires patients to see only psychiatrists.
[*] Alternative models such as collaborative care or capitated arrangements can fund coordination explicitly; under fee-for-service these activities depend on uncompensated effort.
[ ] Payment structures have no relationship to care coordination.

Title: MHA04 Parity enforcement gap
Points: 2
1.  Federal mental health parity law requires insurance plans to cover behavioral health services on terms comparable to medical-surgical services. A state attorney general's office reports that prior-authorization and network-adequacy violations are common in plans operating in the state. Which best explains, in systems terms, why parity-on-paper does not necessarily translate into parity-in-practice?
a)  Parity laws have been repealed.
*b) The gap reflects a governance-versus-enforcement structural distinction — the rules exist, but administrative capacity for monitoring, the burden of proof on patients to identify violations, and the limited consequences for noncompliance leave parity weakly enforced in practice.
c)  Parity has fully achieved its goals.
d)  Parity laws apply only to Medicare beneficiaries.

Title: MHA05 Provider acceptance of Medicaid
Points: 2
1.  In several states, only a minority of psychiatrists accept new Medicaid patients, despite Medicaid being the largest payer of behavioral health services nationally. From a systems perspective, this pattern most clearly reflects:
a)  A coincidence with no relationship to system structure.
*b) A structural mismatch in which Medicaid reimbursement rates, administrative complexity, and the option of cash-pay practice create incentives that reduce the share of providers accepting Medicaid, producing a coverage that does not reliably translate into access for the largest group of insured behavioral health patients.
c)  An indication that Medicaid patients do not need psychiatric care.
d)  A federal requirement that limits Medicaid acceptance.

Title: MHA06 Scope of practice
Points: 2
1.  A state expands the scope of practice for psychiatric nurse practitioners, allowing independent practice and prescribing without physician supervision. Three years later, the share of underserved counties with at least one mental health prescriber has increased meaningfully. From a systems perspective, the most defensible interpretation of this outcome is:
a)  Scope of practice is unrelated to access outcomes.
*b) Scope-of-practice rules are structural levers that shape who is allowed to deliver care; expanding scope alters the effective workforce supply for a given population, particularly in areas where physician supply is constrained, and is one of the few interventions that can change effective access on a multi-year (rather than decade-long) horizon.
c)  Independent practice has no measurable effect on access.
d)  All states have identical scope-of-practice laws.

Title: MHA07 Housing and access
Points: 2
1.  Empirical research has consistently shown that individuals experiencing housing instability have worse mental health outcomes and lower rates of completed mental health care than housed individuals with similar conditions, even when both groups have the same insurance coverage. Which of the following are consistent with a systems-level explanation? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Housing instability disrupts continuity of medication, follow-up, and therapeutic relationships, reducing the rate at which treatment becomes effective.
[*] Many mental health interventions depend on a stable address, predictable schedule, and physical safety that housing provides.
[ ] Housing instability has no relationship to mental health.
[*] Reinforcing dynamics — mental illness contributing to housing loss, housing loss worsening mental illness — can sustain the relationship across time without external intervention.
[ ] Insurance status is the only determinant of mental health care use.

Title: MHA08 Stigma and structural conditions
Points: 2
1.  A state launches an anti-stigma media campaign to encourage adults with mental illness to seek treatment. After two years, surveys show modest improvements in expressed willingness to seek care but no measurable increase in treatment rates. From a systems perspective, the most defensible interpretation is:
a)  Stigma is unrelated to mental health treatment use.
*b) The campaign addressed one barrier (attitudes) but did not change the structural conditions that determine whether stated willingness translates into completed care — provider availability, insurance acceptance, wait times, and clinic accessibility — leaving the campaign's effects to dissipate against unchanged downstream barriers.
c)  Anti-stigma campaigns are forbidden by federal law.
d)  All adults already have access to mental health treatment.

Title: MHA09 Performative response
Points: 2
1.  In response to a high-profile suicide that drew media attention, a health system issues a press release announcing a new "988 awareness initiative," updates its website with crisis line numbers, and adds a banner to clinic waiting areas. From a systems perspective, this response is best characterized as:
a)  A structural intervention addressing the drivers of unmet mental health need
*b) A primarily performative response that signals concern without altering specialty workforce supply, insurance acceptance, parity enforcement, or wait times — the structural features that determine whether a person in crisis can actually obtain care
c)  A change to scope-of-practice laws
d)  An expansion of Medicaid behavioral health benefits

Title: MHA10 Endogenous data in mental health metrics
Points: 2
1.  A state implements a new data system that tracks behavioral health screening in primary care settings. The year after implementation, recorded rates of depression diagnosis rise sharply across the state. A news report concludes that "depression is surging." From a systems perspective, the most defensible interpretation is:
a)  Underlying depression has clearly increased.
*b) The data are partially endogenous to the screening and reporting system; increases in *measured* diagnosis may reflect improved detection and recording of cases that previously went unrecognized, rather than changes in underlying prevalence — though some increase in actual prevalence may also be present.
c)  Screening systems have no effect on measured rates.
d)  All increases in measured diagnoses reflect actual increases in disease.

Title: MHA11 Delay in mental health interventions
Points: 2
1.  A state invests in expanded community-based mental health programs and increased reimbursement for outpatient behavioral health services. Three years later, suicide rates and emergency department visits for psychiatric crises in the state remain essentially unchanged. A legislator proposes ending the funding. From a systems perspective, the strongest argument *against* immediate cuts is:
a)  Programs once funded should never be reduced.
*b) Population-level mental health outcomes reflect long-acting structural conditions and accumulated risk; even effective community-based interventions take years to register in indicators that are sensitive to overall population mental health, and three years is a weak basis for inference about effectiveness.
c)  Community-based programs are unrelated to suicide rates.
d)  Mental health outcomes cannot be measured at the state level.

Title: MHA12 Boundary and individual blame
Points: 2
1.  A health policy commentator argues that the high U.S. mental health treatment gap is "primarily because people don't seek care, even when it's available." From a systems perspective grounded in this course, the strongest critique is:
a)  Patients are required by federal law to seek mental health care.
*b) The framing locates a structurally produced outcome in individual choice; "not seeking care" is shaped by stigma, prior negative experiences with the system, work and childcare constraints, transportation, insurance acceptance, and provider availability — features of the system rather than features of individual decision-making.
c)  Mental health treatment use is unrelated to system features.
d)  All available care is equally accessible to all populations.

Title: MHA13 Reinforcing loop in mental health system
Points: 2
1.  Which of the following best describes a *reinforcing* loop relevant to U.S. mental health access disparities, in the sense used in this course?
a)  Higher specialty workforce supply leads to lower workforce supply.
*b) Low Medicaid reimbursement leads more psychiatrists to limit Medicaid acceptance, which concentrates Medicaid patients with the few accepting providers, which lengthens wait times and worsens working conditions in those settings, which further reduces willingness of new providers to accept Medicaid — a loop that sustains the access gap across time.
c)  Medicaid eligibility automatically expands itself.
d)  Stigma decreases as treatment rates fall.
