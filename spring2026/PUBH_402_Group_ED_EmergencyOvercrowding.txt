Quiz title: PUBH 402 Final Exam — Group ED: Emergency Department Overcrowding
Quiz description: Topic-anchored items for students whose teams analyzed emergency department overcrowding. Items test systems-level reasoning about ED dynamics — supply, demand, coverage, payment, workforce, structural determinants, and the feedback structure that produces persistent overcrowding. Mix of single-answer and multi-select. 2 points each. Suggested timing: 90 seconds for single-answer, 2–2.5 minutes for multi-select.

Title: ED01 Capacity vs throughput
Points: 2
1.  A regional hospital responds to chronic ED overcrowding by adding 30 ED treatment bays and hiring additional ED physicians. Six months later, ED boarding times and length of stay are essentially unchanged. Which is the most defensible systems-level explanation?
a)  The hospital should have added more ED bays.
*b) ED overcrowding is largely driven by inpatient throughput — particularly the inability to move admitted patients out of the ED into staffed inpatient beds — so expanding ED-side capacity without addressing inpatient flow leaves the binding constraint untouched.
c)  ED physician staffing has no relationship to ED throughput.
d)  EMTALA prohibits hospitals from expanding ED capacity.

Title: ED02 EMTALA structural role
Points: 2
1.  A health policy commentator argues that EMTALA "causes ED overcrowding" because it requires emergency departments to screen and stabilize all patients regardless of ability to pay. From a systems perspective, the most accurate response is:
a)  EMTALA is the sole cause of ED overcrowding and should be repealed.
*b) EMTALA is a structural feature that channels otherwise-unmet demand into the ED rather than creating that demand; the underlying drivers are coverage gaps, primary care access, and structural determinants that produce the unmet demand in the first place.
c)  EMTALA has no effect on ED utilization patterns.
d)  EMTALA applies only to Medicare-enrolled patients.

Title: ED03 Coverage expansion and ED use
Points: 2
1.  After a state expands Medicaid eligibility, evidence shows that ED use among newly enrolled adults does not fall and may modestly rise in the short term. Which of the following are consistent with a systems-level explanation of this finding? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Newly insured patients face the same primary care supply constraints as before — coverage does not create access on its own.
[*] Coverage gains can increase health care use overall, including ED use, before primary care relationships stabilize.
[ ] Medicaid expansion has been shown to eliminate all ED use.
[*] Many ED visits reflect conditions for which timely outpatient care is not actually available, regardless of insurance status.
[ ] Newly insured patients are required by federal law to use the ED.

Title: ED04 Boarding and post-acute capacity
Points: 2
1.  A safety-net hospital reports that admitted patients frequently wait 18–36 hours in the ED for an inpatient bed ("ED boarding"). Discharge planning data show that a large share of inpatient beds are occupied by patients who are medically ready for discharge but have no available skilled nursing facility, home health, or post-acute placement. Which best describes the structural relationship?
a)  ED boarding is caused by inadequate ED staffing alone.
*b) ED boarding is propagated upstream from a downstream bottleneck: limited post-acute capacity reduces inpatient turnover, which reduces available beds for ED admissions, which holds patients in the ED.
c)  ED boarding is unrelated to inpatient throughput.
d)  Post-acute capacity has no effect on hospital operations.

Title: ED05 Primary care access drivers
Points: 2
1.  A team analyzing ED overcrowding finds that low-income patients in their region report difficulty obtaining timely primary care appointments despite a sufficient *count* of primary care clinicians per capita. Which of the following are plausible structural explanations consistent with a systems analysis? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Many primary care clinicians limit the share of new Medicaid patients they accept due to reimbursement levels and administrative burden.
[*] Clinic hours, transportation, and language access create non-financial barriers that vary across patient populations.
[*] Continuity of care is shaped by appointment availability, not just clinician supply, and same-week availability for non-acute concerns is often limited.
[ ] All primary care clinicians are required by law to serve Medicaid patients equally.
[ ] Primary care clinician count is the only relevant factor in primary care access.

Title: ED06 Fee-for-service and prevention
Points: 2
1.  A team's analysis identifies fee-for-service payment as a structural driver of ED overcrowding. Which is the most accurate explanation of the causal pathway in systems terms?
a)  Fee-for-service payment directly reduces the number of EDs in operation.
*b) Fee-for-service rewards volume of treatment over investment in prevention and care coordination, weakening the upstream system features that would reduce avoidable acute episodes — the effects on ED demand operate with delay through prevention and chronic disease management, not directly.
c)  Fee-for-service requires patients to use the ED for routine care.
d)  Fee-for-service payment applies only to Medicaid patients.

Title: ED07 Nursing workforce and bed capacity
Points: 2
1.  In a regional health system, "hospital bed capacity" measured by physical beds is roughly stable, but functional staffed bed capacity has declined over the past three years. ED boarding has worsened over the same period. Which of the following are consistent with a systems-level explanation? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Nursing workforce shortages reduce the number of beds that can be safely staffed at any given time, regardless of physical bed count.
[*] Reduced functional capacity propagates into ED boarding because admitted patients cannot move into unstaffed beds.
[ ] Physical bed count is the only meaningful measure of hospital capacity.
[*] Reinforcing dynamics — turnover increasing load on remaining staff, accelerating further departures — can sustain functional capacity decline even when recruitment efforts are underway.
[ ] Nursing staffing has no relationship to ED operations.

Title: ED08 Structural determinants and ED demand
Points: 2
1.  An analysis of ED utilization across a metropolitan area finds that patients experiencing housing instability use EDs at substantially higher rates than housed patients with similar clinical conditions. Which of the following are consistent with a systems-level account? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Housing instability disrupts continuity of medication, follow-up, and outpatient care, increasing the rate at which manageable conditions become acute.
[*] Patients without stable housing may rely on the ED for needs (warmth, safety, basic care access) that the outpatient system is not structured to address.
[ ] Housing instability has no measurable relationship to health care utilization.
[*] EMTALA and 24/7 availability make the ED one of the few care settings reliably accessible to patients without stable housing.
[ ] ED use among unhoused patients is fully explained by individual choices and is not structurally produced.

Title: ED09 Performative responses
Points: 2
1.  In response to public concern about ED wait times, a hospital launches a public information campaign encouraging patients to "use the ED only for true emergencies" and to "consider urgent care alternatives." From a systems perspective, this response is best characterized as:
a)  A structural intervention addressing the drivers of ED demand
*b) A primarily performative response that signals concern without altering coverage gaps, primary care access, post-acute capacity, or payment structures that channel demand to the ED
c)  A change to EMTALA requirements
d)  A structural change to inpatient throughput

Title: ED10 Reinforcing loop in ED system
Points: 2
1.  Which of the following best describes a *reinforcing* loop relevant to ED overcrowding, in the sense used in this course?
a)  More EDs open in a region, leading to fewer ED visits per ED.
*b) Nursing turnover increases workload on remaining nurses, which accelerates burnout and further turnover, reducing functional bed capacity, increasing ED boarding, and worsening working conditions for ED nurses — feeding back into more turnover.
c)  Higher ED visit volume causes hospitals to reduce the number of beds.
d)  EMTALA requirements automatically reduce themselves over time.

Title: ED11 Leverage point identification
Points: 2
1.  A team identifies four candidate intervention points to reduce ED overcrowding. Which two are most plausibly STRUCTURAL leverage points rather than symptomatic responses? (Select two.)
[*] Expanding post-acute and skilled nursing facility capacity to improve inpatient throughput
[ ] Posting "use the ED appropriately" signs in waiting rooms
[*] Extending Medicaid postpartum or chronic disease coverage to stabilize continuity of outpatient care
[ ] Adding pamphlets about urgent care alternatives at hospital exits

Title: ED12 Boundary and individual blame
Points: 2
1.  A health system administrator argues that ED overcrowding is "primarily a problem of patients using the ED inappropriately for non-emergent conditions." From a systems perspective grounded in this course, the strongest critique is:
a)  Patients are required to use the ED only for emergencies under federal law.
*b) The framing locates a structurally produced outcome in individual behavior; "non-emergent" ED use largely reflects coverage gaps, primary care access barriers, and the ED's role as the only reliably accessible care setting for many patients, all of which are system features rather than individual choices.
c)  ED use patterns are unrelated to insurance or primary care.
d)  Patients always make optimal decisions about where to seek care.


Title: ED13 Delay dynamics in ED interventions
Points: 2
1.  A state invests in expanded primary care infrastructure and Medicaid postpartum coverage extension as part of a strategy to reduce ED overcrowding. Two years later, ED visit rates in the affected counties are essentially unchanged. Legislators consider scaling back funding. From a systems perspective, the strongest argument *against* immediate cuts is:
a)  Programs once funded should never be reduced.
*b) The relevant outcomes — chronic disease management, continuity of outpatient care, avoidable acute episodes — are shaped by long-acting structural conditions and accumulated risk; effects on ED demand operate with substantial delay, and short-term flatness in ED utilization is a weak basis for inferring program failure.
c)  ED visit rates are unrelated to primary care or coverage policy.
d)  Medicaid policy changes have immediate effects on ED utilization within weeks.

Title: ED14 Triage and acuity mix
Points: 2
1.  A health system reports that a substantial share of ED visits are categorized as "low acuity" or "non-emergent" by triage criteria. An administrator argues that solving overcrowding requires "diverting these patients elsewhere." From a systems perspective, which of the following are reasonable concerns about this framing? (Select all that apply.)
[*] "Non-emergent" classifications are made retrospectively or at triage and may not reflect what was reasonably knowable to the patient at the time of seeking care.
[*] Even if low-acuity visits were redirected, the ED would remain crowded if inpatient throughput, boarding, and admitted-patient flow remain unchanged — low-acuity volume is often not the binding constraint.
[ ] All ED visits classified as low-acuity are unambiguous misuse of the ED.
[*] "Diverting" requires that an accessible alternative actually exists for the patient population in question; coverage, hours, transportation, and acceptance of Medicaid all shape whether that alternative is real.
[ ] Triage acuity classifications are determined by federal law and cannot be changed.

Title: ED15 Endogenous data in ED metrics
Points: 2
1.  A hospital implements a new electronic triage workflow that automatically time-stamps patient arrival, triage, and provider assessment. In the year after implementation, reported "door-to-provider time" rises substantially, while patient experience scores and clinical outcomes are unchanged. From a systems perspective, the most defensible interpretation is:
a)  Door-to-provider time has clearly worsened in clinical reality.
*b) The data are partially endogenous to the measurement system; improved time-stamping captures delays that previously went unrecorded, so the increase in *measured* door-to-provider time may reflect changes in surveillance and classification rather than changes in underlying ED operations.
c)  Triage workflows have no effect on measured metrics.
d)  Patient experience scores always track operational metrics directly.

Title: ED16 Boundary and downstream framing
Points: 2
1.  A team analyzing ED overcrowding draws a system boundary that includes ED staffing, ED workflow, triage processes, and bed management within the hospital, but excludes primary care access, Medicaid eligibility, post-acute care capacity, and structural determinants of demand. Which is the strongest critique of this boundary choice from a systems perspective?
a)  Boundaries should always be as wide as possible.
*b) The boundary frames ED overcrowding as primarily a workflow and operations problem inside the ED, when most of the structural drivers of ED demand and inpatient throughput originate outside the ED itself; this boundary largely guarantees the analysis cannot explain why the problem persists across hospitals and over time.
c)  ED overcrowding is fully explained by ED-internal workflow.
d)  Boundary choices have no effect on what an analysis can explain.
