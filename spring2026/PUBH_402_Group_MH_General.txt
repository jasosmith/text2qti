Mix of single-answer and multi-select. 2 points each. Suggested timing: 90 sec single-answer, 2–2.5 min multi-select. Recommended pull: 6 items per student.

Title: MH01 Persistent racial disparity
Points: 2
1.  Black women in the United States die of pregnancy-related causes at roughly three times the rate of white women, a gap that has persisted across decades despite overall improvements in many other maternal health indicators. From a systems perspective grounded in this course, the most defensible interpretation is:
a)  The disparity reflects underlying genetic differences in pregnancy risk.
*b) The disparity is produced by reinforcing structural factors — including residential segregation, differential access to high-quality hospitals, provider bias in clinical encounter quality, and postpartum coverage gaps — that operate through multiple pathways and persist because the underlying structure has not changed.
c)  The disparity is fully explained by individual behavioral differences during pregnancy.
d)  The disparity has no measurable connection to system structure.

Title: MH02 Postpartum coverage cliff
Points: 2
1.  In states that have not extended postpartum Medicaid coverage beyond 60 days, what structural dynamic is most directly relevant to maternal mortality, given that a substantial share of pregnancy-related deaths occur after the traditional 60-day postpartum window?
a)  The 60-day limit has no relationship to maternal outcomes because most deaths occur during delivery.
*b) The coverage cliff terminates access to clinical management of conditions (hypertension, mental health, cardiomyopathy) that contribute substantially to late-postpartum deaths, creating a structural gap between the period of clinical risk and the period of insured access.
c)  Postpartum Medicaid is unrelated to clinical management of pregnancy-related conditions.
d)  Pregnancy-related deaths after 60 days are not classified as maternal mortality.

[ ] Late entry to prenatal care has no relationship to system features

Title: MH04 OB deserts and workforce

1.  A growing share of U.S. counties — disproportionately rural — have no obstetric provider and no hospital with labor and delivery services. Which of the following are consistent with a systems-level account of how this geographic pattern affects maternal outcomes? (Select all that apply.)
[*] Travel distance to delivery is a measurable risk factor for adverse intrapartum outcomes.
[*] Hospital labor-and-delivery closures are driven by reinforcing dynamics including unfavorable payer mix, low delivery volume, and malpractice insurance costs.
[ ] Maternal outcomes are unrelated to where patients deliver.
[*] Workforce shortage and facility loss tend to concentrate in the same regions, compounding access barriers.
[ ] All counties are required by federal law to maintain obstetric services.

Title: MH05 Provider bias and quality
Points: 2
1.  Empirical research has documented that Black patients report having symptoms dismissed or pain undertreated during pregnancy and delivery at higher rates than white patients. From a systems perspective, the most defensible characterization of how provider bias relates to maternal outcomes is:
a)  Provider bias is an individual character flaw unrelated to system structure.
*b) Provider bias is a feature of the clinical encounter that is produced and reinforced by training, institutional culture, and accountability structures, and that operates as one mechanism through which structural inequity translates into differential clinical care and outcomes.
c)  Provider bias has no documented effect on clinical decision-making.
d)  Provider bias is fully addressed by current quality reporting systems.

Title: MH06 Severe maternal morbidity vs mortality
Points: 2
1.  Severe maternal morbidity (SMM) — life-threatening complications of pregnancy and delivery — affects roughly 50,000 to 60,000 U.S. patients annually, while pregnancy-related deaths number in the hundreds. From a systems perspective, why is SMM an important indicator alongside mortality?
a)  SMM is a less reliable measure than mortality.
*b) SMM is far more common than death and reflects similar underlying system failures, providing a higher-volume signal of where care delivery and structural factors are producing harm — making SMM useful for identifying patterns that mortality alone is too rare to detect.
c)  SMM has no relationship to mortality.
d)  SMM is measured only in research settings.

Title: MH08 Doula and midwifery scope
Points: 2
1.  Several states have moved to expand Medicaid coverage of doula services and to broaden midwifery scope of practice. From a systems perspective, the strongest argument that these changes could improve maternal outcomes is that:
a)  Doulas and midwives can replace all obstetric care.
*b) These changes alter who is part of the maternal care workforce, what relationships patients have with their care team, and how non-medical support is integrated — addressing engagement, advocacy, and continuity dimensions that the obstetrician-only model has historically underserved.
c)  Doulas and midwives have no role in maternal care.
d)  Scope-of-practice laws are unrelated to workforce composition.

Title: MH09 Delay in maternal interventions
Points: 2
1.  A state launches a maternal mortality review committee and a quality improvement initiative focused on hemorrhage and hypertension protocols. Three years later, severe maternal morbidity rates in the state remain essentially unchanged at the population level. A legislator proposes ending funding. From a systems perspective, the strongest argument *against* immediate cuts is:
a)  Maternal mortality programs should never be reduced.
*b) Population-level maternal outcomes reflect long-acting structural conditions; even effective clinical protocol interventions accumulate slowly across the volume of deliveries needed to register population-level change, and three years is a weak basis for inference about effectiveness.
c)  Quality improvement programs have no effect on maternal outcomes.
d)  Severe maternal morbidity cannot be measured at the state level.

Title: MH11 Boundary and individual blame
Points: 2
1.  A health policy commentator argues that high U.S. maternal mortality rates are "primarily the result of women entering pregnancy with poor health behaviors and chronic conditions." From a systems perspective grounded in this course, the strongest critique is:
a)  Pregnant patients are required to adopt specific health behaviors under federal law.
*b) The framing locates a structurally produced outcome in individual behavior; the prevalence of pre-pregnancy chronic conditions is itself shaped by structural factors (food access, economic security, neighborhood environment, prior healthcare access) that vary systematically across populations and are not freely chosen.
c)  Chronic conditions have no relationship to maternal outcomes.
d)  Maternal mortality is fully explained by individual choices.

Title: MH12 Reinforcing loop in maternal system
Points: 2
1.  Which of the following best describes a *reinforcing* loop relevant to U.S. maternal health disparities, in the sense used in this course?
a)  Lower maternal mortality leads to more births, which lowers maternal mortality further.
*b) Hospitals serving segregated communities receive less revenue per delivery, which constrains staffing and quality investment, which produces worse outcomes, which reduces patient trust and willingness to engage with care, which weakens the patient base and revenue further — a structural loop that sustains disparities across institutions.
c)  Provider bias automatically reduces itself through clinical exposure.
d)  EMTALA requirements automatically resolve maternal mortality disparities.

Title: MH13 Coverage as floor not ceiling
Points: 2
1.  A graduate student argues that "if every pregnant person had Medicaid coverage from conception through one year postpartum, maternal mortality disparities would disappear." Which set of considerations most directly complicates this claim from a systems perspective?
[*] Coverage addresses one set of barriers (financial access, continuity) but does not change clinical encounter quality, provider bias, hospital quality variation, or workforce geography.
[*] Structural inequities in housing, environmental exposure, and economic security operate independently of insurance coverage and shape pre-pregnancy and pregnancy health.
[ ] Coverage has been shown to worsen maternal outcomes overall.
[*] The structural factors producing disparities operate through multiple reinforcing pathways; addressing one input does not necessarily disrupt the loop.
[ ] Coverage is irrelevant to maternal outcomes.
