Title: ED-C-T1 Map — outcome variable choice and door-to-provider time
Points: 2
1.  Your team's final system map used "ED door-to-provider time" as the central outcome variable, with most causal pathways pointing toward it. From a systems perspective, what is the most defensible critique of using a workflow-internal metric like door-to-provider time as the central outcome of a system map of ED overcrowding?
a)  Door-to-provider time is not a measurable indicator of ED operations.
*b) Door-to-provider time captures one ED-internal performance dimension but does not represent the upstream system features (coverage, primary care access, structural determinants) that produce ED demand; using it as the central outcome tends to channel the analysis toward workflow and staffing solutions, while leaving the structural drivers of demand outside the analytic frame.
c)  ED metrics cannot appear in any system analysis.
d)  Door-to-provider time is the only legitimate measure of ED performance.

Title: ED-C-T2 Map — element list narrowness
Points: 2
1.  Your team's final element list focused on workforce, hospital infrastructure, payment, and a small number of structural elements (income security, racialized residential segregation). The map did not include preventive service availability, public health surveillance capacity, transportation access, or inequitable access to health-promoting resources. From a systems perspective, what is the most defensible critique of this element selection given the topic of ED overcrowding?
a)  Element selection is unrelated to what a map can explain.
*b) The selected elements emphasize the *supply side* of care delivery (workforce, beds, payment) but underrepresent the *demand-generating* features of the system (prevention, surveillance, transportation, and the broader inequitable access pathways) that determine why ED demand is what it is — leaving the map structurally limited in its ability to explain why ED overcrowding persists.
c)  The team included too many elements for analysis.
d)  All elements have equal causal weight regardless of topic.

Title: ED-C-T3 Map — flow gridlock loop
Points: 2
1.  Your team's final system map identified an "R2 Flow Gridlock" loop with the description that lower hospital bed capacity increases ED congestion, and higher ED congestion reduces inpatient turnover, lowering functional bed capacity over time. From a systems perspective, this is correctly identified as a *reinforcing* loop rather than a balancing loop because:
a)  All loops involving hospital beds are reinforcing.
*b) The loop has no internal mechanism that resists or reverses the trend — reduced bed capacity leads to more ED congestion, which further reduces functional bed capacity, amplifying the original change rather than counteracting it. A balancing loop would require a mechanism in the loop that pushes the system back toward equilibrium.
c)  Reinforcing loops always involve more than two elements.
d)  Bed capacity is unrelated to ED operations.

Title: ED-C-T4 Map — segregation pathway placement
Points: 2
1.  Your team's final system map identified racialized residential segregation as a structural inequity element, with arrows showing that segregation reduces primary care facility availability and primary care clinician supply over long delays. From a systems perspective, what is the most defensible refinement of this causal pathway?
a)  Segregation has no relationship to primary care infrastructure.
*b) The pathways drawn are accurate but partial; segregation also operates through other elements not in the map (housing instability, environmental exposure, economic security, hospital quality variation across segregated communities) that shape ED demand. Including only the primary care infrastructure pathway represents one true causal channel while leaving most of how segregation produces ED utilization disparities outside the analysis.
c)  Segregation should not appear in any health system map.
d)  Long delays cannot be represented in system maps.
